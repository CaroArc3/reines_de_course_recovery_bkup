By Ron Parker

A cursory glance at most five-cross pedigrees will point out how breeders have allowed Raise a Native blood to permeate the breed, and while he is hardly the sole culprit it is no coincidence that the soundness of the breed has deteriorated dangerously over the past few decades as his influence has spread.

A foal of 1961 by Native Dancer out of the Case Ace mare Raise You, Raise a Native only raced four times but managed to win them all while setting or equaling track records at Aqueduct for both five and five-and-one-half furlongs.

Not surprisingly he was all the buzz, despite the fact that he had yet to race beyond five-and-one-half furlongs, but during a workout in anticipation of the 1963 Sanford--which would have been his fifth start as a two-year-old--he ruptured a tendon sheath of his near foreleg and was promptly retired to stud.  But his accomplishments earned him the two-year-old male championship.

Although there were high hopes for him as a stallion, writer Timothy T. Capps would note some 20 years later that "soundness is a problem for this line."

In an article in the March 13, 1985 Thoroughbred Record, in itself an excerpt from the book Contemporary Stallions, Capps wrote:

"Raise a Native's colts have been better than his fillies as a rule, although he has sired several good fillies, and his sons have also done well as progenitors of good racing females.  Raisela, Bundler, and Where You Lead are perhaps the best of his fillies on the race track, and his daughters are certainly prized as broodmare prospects, having already produced more than 30 stakes winners, including Danzatore, Nijinsky's Secret, Wickker, and Flying Partner.

"As physical types, the Raise a Natives are also varied, ranging from compact, sometimes muscular, sometimes light-boned horses, to big, coarse, powerful specimens.  They are frequently rather straight-pasterned, and many either turn out in their forelegs or have offset knees.  These characteristics lead to osselets and other ankle or knee problems, and it is fair to say that Raise a Native tends to sire a lot of horses which have difficulty staying sound, as did he and several of his immediate ancestors.  This is especially noticeable among those which are light-boned, for they often have to contend with the big, blocky bodies that are typical of the line.  Majestic Prince is an example of this, and he passed this on to many of his offspring."

Certainly Raise a Native's immediate progeny enjoyed some success, most notably the ill-fated Alydar and of course Majestic Prince.  However one wonders about the regard some of his offspring were held in when their racing careers ended.  Clear Choice was a Grade 1 winner, but he ended up as a sire in Australia.  Highland Park was a Grade 2 winner whose stud career took him to New Mexico.

As Capps pointed out, the success of the line leans toward the distaff side, as evidenced by Raise a Native's status as fifth leading broodmare sire of 1982 and third in that category in 1984.  His son Exclusive Native gave the world 1980 Kentucky Derby winner Genuine Risk, the first filly in 65 years to win the Derby.

All of the above was written before the rise to power – and almost world dominance along with Northern Dancer of Raise a Native’s most influential son, Mr. Prospector.  Mr. Prospector, especially via lines like Fappiano and Gone West, are among the most coveted in all of racing.  And while Mr. Prospector himself was a pure sprinter with Raise a Native’s speed, he was also out of a mare by the sound, durable and good-boned Nashua and was tail-female to the immensely successful sire-source mare, Frizette via Myrtlewood, the same branch that gave us Seattle Slew.  Because some of the Mr. Prospectors threw to the Nashua side (soundness), they were able to sustain more stress than other more fragile souls.  But when Mr. Prospector began showing up in doubles, then we really started to feel the pain.  Yet of course before there were doubles of Mr. Prospector, there were already doubles of Raise a Native.

The problem with Raise a Native is the old axiom about too much of a good thing.  A single line of his blood usually didn't court disaster unless mixed with too many other unsound influences leading to the inevitable rule that unsoundness trumps soundness.

But breeders had other ideas, if one line of Raise a Native was good, then more must certainly be better.  Never mind the increased exposure to "osselets and other ankle or knee problems," if that's a possible problem we can hide it and the pedigree looks good on a catalog page!

The most recent glaring example of this perceived 'more is better' thinking is, of course, Eight Belles, who carried no less than three crosses of Raise a Native (3D x 4S x 4D).  Native Dancer, Raise a Native's suspect sire, contributes four crosses, adding a dose of fragile Dixieland Band through Natalma.  And let's not leave out Eight Belle's sire, Unbridled's Song, subject of the first article in this series on Unsound Breeding.

Eight Belles sustained compound fractures of both front legs at the fetlock joints prompting everyone with an opinion to blame the jockey, the trainer, the track...everything but the most obvious fact that this was a pedigree saturated with unsoundness.

One of the members of the media who was not led down the blame everything else path was Andy Beyer, writing in the Washington Post.

"...Eight Belles was a tragic manifestation of a problem that is more pronounced every year," Beyer wrote.  "America's breeding industry is producing increasingly fragile thoroughbreds.  They may not break down, but they have shorter and shorter racing careers before going to stud to beget even more fragile offspring.

"The facts are irrefutable.  In 1960, the average U.S. racehorse made 11.3 starts per year.  The number has fallen almost every year, and now the average U.S. thoroughbred races a mere 6.3 times per year.  Almost every trainer whose career spans the decades will acknowledge aren't as robust as they used to be."

Award-winning writer Bill Nack wrote an article for ESPN.COM (you can find it at http://sports.espn.go.com/sports/horse/triplecrown08/columns/story?columnist=nack_bill&id=3399004) on the Eight Belles tragedy, an article that should have garnered him another award, but of course telling it like it is doesn't win awards from the very community being criticized.

In the article, Ellen points out that Eight Belles broke down "right where Raise a Native was the weakest, right in the ankles, and everybody acts like they don't know what caused this filly to break down."

Nack also talked to Three Chimneys pedigree advisor Anne Peters, who said that blaming it on Raise a Native was "absurd."

Peters flatly stated that Eight Belles was a mating "designed to add soundness through other sources.  The fact of the matter is, the filly was sound."

But as Nack pointed out, Peters was defending a statement she made less than two weeks earlier to the Wall Street Journal, admitting that "there's a blessing and a curse" involved in breeding descendents of Native Dancer.

If Eight Belles were an isolated incident, maybe the argument that it was a freak occurrence might carry some weight, but this year's Triple Crown trail seemed like more of a Cripple Crown trail.

Consider Terrain, who pulled up approaching the three-eighths marker in the Iowa Derby and was vanned off the track.  Terrain is another example of a Raise a Native trifecta: 4D x 5S x 6S which includes a double of Mr. Prospector.  As for Terrain, he was promptly euthanized due to the severity of a fractured sesamoid and condylar fracture in his right front leg.

"He warmed up well, started off nice," trainer Al Stall Jr. said.  "Robby [jockey Robby Albarado] said he was like butter, but down the backside something went wrong."

Raise a Native doubles knocked several hopefuls off the Triple Crown trail.

Old Fashioned was one of the early top contenders before he was sidelined with "a non-displaced slab fracture to his right knee" that was presumably sustained while finishing second in the Arkansas Derby.  Another son of Unbridled's Song (see Eight Belles), Old Fashioned carries a 4D x 5S cross of Raise a Native.

Point Encounter may or may not return to the races after trainer Carla Gaines took him out of training after one (count 'em, 1) start, a maiden victory the day after Christmas last year at Santa Anita.  He hasn't raced since.

Daily Racing Form reported that "Gaines declined to be specific regarding the exact nature of the injury, and did not say if the colt hurt himself in the race or while training."

Gaines offered that "the things [injuries] that are showing up, who knows when they start?"

Maybe "these things" started with a 5S x 5S cross of Raise a Native (and a 6S x 6S x 6D dash of "blessing and a curse" Native Dancer).  Add a touch of unsound Danzig and you have a recipe for b-r-e-a-k-d-o-w-n.

Believe in Hope was a Triple Crown aspirant who finished third to Street Hero in the Norfolk Stakes as a two-year-old but never got on the trail after trainer Ron Ellis said he was "just not acting right."  Ellis said he would give him "some time to recoup." 

The Norfolk was run on September 28, 2008 and the colt hasn't raced since.  Sure takes a lot of time these days to "recoup."

Turning our attention away from the three-year-olds that won't make it to the handicap ranks, Necessary Evil was another, though currently non-fatal, example of the fragility three crosses of Raise a Native (5S x 5D x 6D) brings to the table.

The two-year-old filly fractured her left front ankle during the running of the Del Mar Debutante.

"We're not sure when it happened," said David Greathouse of co-owner Glencrest Farm, "but she was bumped right after the start, and that certainly may have contributed."

The prognosis for a recovery is "excellent" according to Greathouse, let's just hope that no one "bumps" her if she returns to racing. 

On the other hand, they can always breed her; maybe they can get a foal with four or five crosses, wouldn't that be wonderful for the breed?

Radio ads for music stations like to say that "the hits just keep on coming," and when it comes to doubling, much less tripling, Raise a Native blood, so do the injuries.

Mi Sueno, who was the top-ranked two-year-old filly in California a couple of months ago, suffered a condylar fracture of the right cannon bone in a workout at Hollywood Park on September 29.

"She was going so easy; it happened galloping out" trainer Eric Gulliot said.  "You could see by the time she got back to the barn that something was going on."

What might have been going on was a 4S x 5D Raise a Native cross combined with several runners who broke down at young ages, i.e., What a Pleasure, Storm Cat, and Sassy Pants.

Grazen, the 4-to-5 favorite, looked like the winner of the California Cup Classic at Santa Anita on October 3 when he was pulled up and failed to finish the race.

Vanned off the track, steward Kim Sawyer said that the three-year-old had a "soft-tissue" injury, which could be a plausible explanation since that is the type of injury that "safe" artificial surfaces seem to have created.

Grazen, who was fitted with a splint that took the weight off his right foreleg, carries Raise a Native 3S x 5D in combination with a dose of Danzig that again compounds the soundness problem.

Singularly, Raise a Native is not the kiss of death, but combining intrinsic unsoundness with more unsoundness without regard to the welfare of the animal is not only another step in the de-evolution of the breed, it’s downright criminal.

By Ellen Parker

Too often in recent months, we have gotten e-mails, phone calls, etc. from folks reminding us that Raise a Native bowed a tendon and wondering why we hate him so.   To the first comment, we are well aware he bowed a tendon but that has nothing to do with the ankles he inherited from Native Dancer that he tends to pass on.

As to hating him…that’s almost funny.  He was a personal favorite.  Whenever we went to visit his old barn-mate “Micky” (Nashua), we also stopped to see Raise, asking Clem Brooks to bring him out as well when possible.  “Raise” always came out of his stall in pure ham form, often on his hind legs, snorting and putting on a show.  But all show it was, for he was no man eater.  I often fed him a lump of sugar and his big, soft eyes would darken in wonder when I spoke softly to him.  Hate him?  Hells bells, people, I loved the big lug.

I loved Unbridled, too – the gorgeous thing - and Unbridled’s Song – another awe-inspiring specimen.  Empire Maker remains one of our all-time favorites and Street Cry earns more respect from us with every passing day. 

Yet I could and can look at each of them and love them and still know that beneath the charming personas, the gleaming coats and regal bearing lies a deadly combination of speed and fragility.  In one dose it can make American speed realize classic potential, in two it can create potential problems, and in threes it brings us Eight Belles and Pine Islands.  Who among you can not see why too much of a good thing not only narrows the gene pool but reduces it to that of a lesser animal? 

Who among you wants to continue to witness these atrocities of greed?  For that is, in fact, the problem.  Big, fast, early-maturing horses sell well.  And when a breeder looks you in the eye and tells you he’d rather have a sales topper than a runner who carries his own silks because, “I can’t make any money racing”, what he is really saying is that he does not believe in the product he produces but that it’s all right for some other poor schmuck to pay $5 million for it.  This has become the legacy of Native Dancer/Raise a Native/Mr. Prospector inbreeding.

It is not fair to the breed, it is not fair to the horses of this tail-male line, many of whom have been wonderful creatures, and it is not fair to the man in the street who goes to the sale hoping to buy a nice racehorse who might actually stay at the track long enough to earn a few bucks.  For those among you who suggest we hate Raise a Native, we ask the following, “Would you not prefer to see him remembered as part of the solution rather than part of the problem?”  Because right now that’s what he is.  And until breeders begin to take a responsible look at the matings they affect each year, it’s only going to get worse.

We can’t put arses in seats at racetracks as it is because our horses don’t stay at the track long enough to gain a following.  And all too often when people do go to the track, they see a horse fatally injured.  If horses continue to break down and have fewer and fewer starts as they have in the last 20-30 years, just how many more racetracks will close?  Then tell me who is going to line up to buy any ‘commercial’ racehorse – you know, that animal the guy who bred him can’t make money with himself!
